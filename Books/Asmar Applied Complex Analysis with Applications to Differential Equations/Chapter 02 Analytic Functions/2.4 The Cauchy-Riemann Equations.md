


> [!review]
> 1. The existence of a complex derivative is far more restrictive than the existence of partial derivatives. What hidden relationship between the real and imaginary parts of $f=u+$ iv can be uncovered by computing $f^{\prime}\left(z_0\right)$ along two different approach paths and forcing the results to agree, and what can be said about the continuity of those partial derivatives? Prove your answer.
> 2. Conversely, what conditions on $u$ and $v$ are sufficient to guarantee that $f=u+i v$ is analytic on an open set, and why is this converse form often more useful in applications than the forward direction?



##### problem 1

The hidden relationship is that, if

$$
f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)
$$

is analytic, then the real and imaginary parts cannot vary independently. They must satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

Moreover, in the presentation of this section, the partial derivatives

$$
u_x,\quad u_y,\quad v_x,\quad v_y
$$

are continuous.

The reason these relations appear is that the derivative

$$
f'(z_0)
$$

must have the same value no matter how $z$ approaches $z_0$. Write

$$
z_0=x_0+iy_0.
$$

Since $f$ is analytic at $z_0$, the derivative exists:

$$
f'(z_0)
=\lim_{z\to z_0}\frac{f(z)-f(z_0)}{z-z_0}
=\lim_{\Delta z\to 0}\frac{f(z_0+\Delta z)-f(z_0)}{\Delta z}.
$$

Now approach $z_0$ along the $x$-direction, so that

$$
\Delta z=\Delta x.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(z_0)
&=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{f(x_0+\Delta x+iy_0)-f(x_0+iy_0)}{\Delta x}\\
&=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\bigl(u(x_0+\Delta x,y_0)+iv(x_0+\Delta x,y_0)\bigr)-\bigl(u(x_0,y_0)+iv(x_0,y_0)\bigr)}{\Delta x}\\
&=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\left(\frac{u(x_0+\Delta x,y_0)-u(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta x}+i\,\frac{v(x_0+\Delta x,y_0)-v(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta x}\right)\\
&=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{u(x_0+\Delta x,y_0)-u(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta x}
+i\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{v(x_0+\Delta x,y_0)-v(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta x}\\
&=u_x(x_0,y_0)+iv_x(x_0,y_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

Next approach $z_0$ along the $y$-direction, so that

$$
\Delta z=i\Delta y.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(z_0)
&=\lim_{\Delta y\to 0}\frac{f(x_0+i(y_0+\Delta y))-f(x_0+iy_0)}{i\Delta y}\\
&=\lim_{\Delta y\to 0}\frac{\bigl(u(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)+iv(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)\bigr)-\bigl(u(x_0,y_0)+iv(x_0,y_0)\bigr)}{i\Delta y}\\
&=\lim_{\Delta y\to 0}\left(\frac{u(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)-u(x_0,y_0)}{i\Delta y}+i\,\frac{v(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)-v(x_0,y_0)}{i\Delta y}\right)\\
&=\lim_{\Delta y\to 0}\left(-i\,\frac{u(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)-u(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta y}+\frac{v(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)-v(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta y}\right)\\
&=\lim_{\Delta y\to 0}\frac{v(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)-v(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta y}
-i\lim_{\Delta y\to 0}\frac{u(x_0,y_0+\Delta y)-u(x_0,y_0)}{\Delta y}\\
&=v_y(x_0,y_0)-iu_y(x_0,y_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

But both computations represent the same complex number $f'(z_0)$. Therefore

$$
u_x(x_0,y_0)+iv_x(x_0,y_0)=v_y(x_0,y_0)-iu_y(x_0,y_0).
$$

Equating real and imaginary parts gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x(x_0,y_0)&=v_y(x_0,y_0),\\
v_x(x_0,y_0)&=-u_y(x_0,y_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

That is,

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

So the existence of the complex derivative forces a very rigid compatibility between the real and imaginary parts. This is exactly the hidden relation the review question is asking about.

As for continuity, the section states that because $f$ is analytic, $f'$ is continuous. From the two formulas

$$
f'(z)=u_x(x,y)+iv_x(x,y)
$$

and

$$
f'(z)=v_y(x,y)-iu_y(x,y),
$$

it follows that

$$
u_x,\quad v_x,\quad u_y,\quad v_y
$$

are all continuous.

##### problem 2

Conversely, a sufficient condition for

$$
f=u+iv
$$

to be analytic on an open set $S$ is that the partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ are continuous on $S$ and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations throughout $S$:

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

Under these hypotheses, $f$ is analytic on $S$.

This converse form is often more useful in applications than the forward direction because, in practice, it is usually much easier to compute

$$
u_x,\quad u_y,\quad v_x,\quad v_y
$$

and check

$$
u_x=v_y,
\qquad
u_y=-v_x,
$$

than it is to return to the definition

$$
f'(z_0)=\lim_{\Delta z\to 0}\frac{f(z_0+\Delta z)-f(z_0)}{\Delta z}
$$

and prove directly that this limit exists and is independent of the way $\Delta z$ tends to $0$.

So the forward direction explains what analyticity forces, while the converse gives a practical test for analyticity. That is why, as the article says, the converse is especially useful in applications: to establish that a function is analytic, one often verifies the Cauchy-Riemann equations together with continuity of the partial derivatives rather than attacking the complex difference quotient directly.

The fact that the derivative of $\bar{z}$ does not exist (Example 4, Section 2.3) should tell you that there is something special about complex-valued functions with derivatives. As you will see, the existence of the derivative implies special relationships between the real and imaginary parts of the functions. These relationships are known as the Cauchy-Riemann equations, which we now derive.

Suppose that $f(z)=f(x+i y)=u(x, y)+i v(x, y)$ is analytic in an open set $S$ and let $z_{0}=x_{0}+i y_{0}=\left(x_{0}, y_{0}\right)$ be a point in $S$. Consequently, the derivative at $z_{0}$ exists and is given by

$$

To derive the Cauchy-Riemann equations, we will simply compute this limit Suppose that $z$ approaches $z_{0}$ along the direction of the $x$-axis, as in _Figure 1_ . 
Then $z=z_{0}+\Delta x=\left(x_{0}+\Delta x, y_{0}\right), \Delta z=z-z_{0}=\Delta x$, and (1) becomes

$$

& =\lim _{\Delta x \rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{u\left(x_{0}+\Delta x, y_{0}\right)-u\left(x_{0}, y_{0}\right)}{\Delta x}+i \frac{v\left(x_{0}+\Delta x, y_{0}\right)-v\left(x_{0}, y_{0}\right)}{\Delta x}\right) \\

Recognizing the partial derivatives with respect to $y$ of $v$ and $u$, we obtain

$$
f^{\prime}\left(z_{0}\right)=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\left(x_{0}, y_{0}\right)-i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\left(x_{0}, y_{0}\right),
$$

which is this time an expression of the derivative of $f$ in terms of the partial derivatives with respect to $y$ of $u$ and $v$. Now comes the startling result. Equate real and imaginary parts in (3) and (5) and get

> [!equation] The Cauchy-Reimann Equations
> 
> $$
> \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \quad \text { and } \quad \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=-\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}
> $$
> 

Thus, in order for $f$ to be analytic, $u$ and $v$ must satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations. Moreover, since $f$ is analytic, $f^{\prime}$ is continuous, and it follows from (3) and (5) and Theorem 4(iv), Section 2.2, that $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$, and $\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$ are all continuous. The converse of these statements is also true. In applications, we will need the converse, since to establish the analyticity of a function, it is often easier to check the Cauchy-Riemann equations and the continuity of the partial derivatives. Because the proof of the converse requires advanced topics from calculus of several variables, we postpone it until Section 2.6, where it will be treated in detail. Let us now summarize our discussion
and proceed with the applications. To simplify the notation, we will denote partial derivatives with subscripts, $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=u_{x}, \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=u_{y}$, and so on.




> [!review]
> 1. How can the analyticity of $f=u+i v$ on an open set be characterized in terms of the partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ ?
> 2. If $f=u+i v$ is analytic on an open set, how can the derivative $f^{\prime}(z)$ be expressed in terms of the partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ ? Give two equivalent expressions.


##### problem 1

The analyticity of

$$
f=u+iv
$$

on an open set is characterized by the Cauchy-Riemann theorem: $f$ is analytic on the open set if and only if the partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ are continuous there and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

So analyticity is equivalent to two requirements taken together:

$$
\text{continuity of }u_x,u_y,v_x,v_y
$$

and

$$
u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.
$$

##### problem 2

If

$$
f=u+iv
$$

is analytic on an open set, then its derivative can be written in either of the two equivalent forms

$$
f'(z)=u_x(x,y)+iv_x(x,y)
$$

or

$$
f'(z)=v_y(x,y)-iu_y(x,y).
$$

These two expressions agree because, for an analytic function, the Cauchy-Riemann equations hold:

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v_x=-u_y.
$$

Thus the derivative may be computed either from the partial derivatives with respect to $x$ or from the partial derivatives with respect to $y$.

> [!theorem] Theorem 1: The Cauchy-Reimann Equations
> The function $f(z)=u(x, y)+i v(x, y)$ is analytic in an open set $S$ if and only if the partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ are continuous and satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations
> (7)
> 
> $$
> u_{x}=v_{y} \quad \text { and } \quad u_{y}=-v_{x} .
> $$
> 
> The derivative $f^{\prime}(z)$ is given as either of
> 
> $$
> f^{\prime}(z)=u_{x}(x, y)+i v_{x}(x, y) \text { or } f^{\prime}(z)=v_{y}(x, y)-i u_{y}(x, y) .
> $$
> 

The Cauchy-Riemann equations appeared in 1821 in the early work of Cauchy on integrals of complex-valued functions. Their connection to the existence of the derivative, as stated in Theorem 1, appeared in 1851 in the doctoral dissertation of the great German mathematician, Bernhard Riemann (1826-1866). As it should be obvious to any one who has studied calculus, Riemann's work shaped the development of modern calculus. His study of complex functions was motivated by his work in hydrodynamics, fluid flow and other applied problems. Some of these applications and their connection to the Cauchy-Riemann equations will be explored in the following section and Chapter 6.

> [!exercise] Exercise 1: $e^z$ is entire.
> 
> 1.) Show that $e^{z}$ is entire and
> 
> $$
> \frac{d}{d z} e^{z}=e^{z} .
> $$
> 
> 
> 2.) Show that if $f(z)$ is analytic on an open set, then $e^{f(z)}$ is analytic on that set and

> $$
> \frac{d}{d z} e^{f(z)}=f^{\prime}(z) e^{f(z)}
> $$
> 
> 


##### solution

We treat the two parts separately.

##### problem 1

To show that

$$
e^z
$$

is entire, write

$$
z=x+iy.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^z
&=e^{x+iy}\\
&=e^x e^{iy}\\
&=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y).
\end{aligned}
$$

So the real and imaginary parts are

$$
u(x,y)=e^x\cos y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v(x,y)=e^x\sin y.
$$

Now compute the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^x\cos y)=e^x\cos y,\\
u_y&=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(e^x\cos y)=-e^x\sin y,\\
v_x&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(e^x\sin y)=e^x\sin y,\\
v_y&=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(e^x\sin y)=e^x\cos y.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

The partial derivatives are continuous everywhere in $\mathbb C$, so by the Cauchy-Riemann theorem,

$$
e^z
$$

is analytic everywhere. Hence $e^z$ is entire.

To compute its derivative, use either formula from Theorem 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dz}e^z
&=u_x+iv_x\\
&=e^x\cos y+i e^x\sin y\\
&=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y)\\
&=e^z.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\frac{d}{dz}e^z=e^z.
$$

##### problem 2

Now suppose that

$$
f(z)
$$

is analytic on an open set $S$.

Since part (1) shows that the exponential function is entire, it is analytic at every complex number. Therefore we may compose it with $f$. Because the composition of analytic functions is analytic wherever the inside function is analytic, it follows that

$$
e^{f(z)}
$$

is analytic on $S$.

To compute its derivative, apply the chain rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dz}e^{f(z)}
&=\left(\frac{d}{dw}e^w\right)\bigg|_{w=f(z)}\cdot f'(z)\\
&=e^{f(z)}f'(z).
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence

$$
\frac{d}{dz}e^{f(z)}=f'(z)e^{f(z)}.
$$


++++


Combining the chain rule with the result of Example 1, we see that $e^{f(z)}$ is analytic wherever $f(z)$ is analytic and

$$
\frac{d}{d z} e^{f(z)}=f^{\prime}(z) e^{f(z)}
$$




> [!exercise] Exercise 2: $\sin z$ is entire.
> Show that $\sin z$ is entire and
> 
> $$
> \frac{d}{d z} \sin z=\cos z
> $$
> 




##### solution

Write

$$
z=x+iy.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin z
&=\sin(x+iy)\\
&=\sin x\cos(iy)+\cos x\sin(iy).
\end{aligned}
$$

Now use

$$
\cos(iy)=\cosh y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\sin(iy)=i\sinh y.
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin z
&=\sin x\cosh y+i\cos x\sinh y.
\end{aligned}
$$

So the real and imaginary parts are

$$
u(x,y)=\sin x\cosh y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v(x,y)=\cos x\sinh y.
$$

Now compute the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\sin x\cosh y)=\cos x\cosh y,\\
u_y&=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\sin x\cosh y)=\sin x\sinh y,\\
v_x&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\cos x\sinh y)=-\sin x\sinh y,\\
v_y&=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(\cos x\sinh y)=\cos x\cosh y.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

Since these partial derivatives are continuous everywhere in $\mathbb C$, the Cauchy-Riemann theorem shows that

$$
\sin z
$$

is analytic everywhere. Hence $\sin z$ is entire.

To compute its derivative, use

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dz}\sin z
&=u_x+iv_x\\
&=\cos x\cosh y+i(-\sin x\sinh y)\\
&=\cos x\cosh y-i\sin x\sinh y.
\end{aligned}
$$

But

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos z
&=\cos(x+iy)\\
&=\cos x\cos(iy)-\sin x\sin(iy)\\
&=\cos x\cosh y-\sin x(i\sinh y)\\
&=\cos x\cosh y-i\sin x\sinh y.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{d}{dz}\sin z=\cos z.
$$

> [!exercise] Exercise 3: $\log z$ is analytic except on the branch cut.
> Show that the principal branch of the logarithm, $\log z$, is analytic on $\mathbb{C} \backslash(-\infty, 0]$, and that
> 
> $$
> \frac{d}{d z} \log z=\frac{1}{z}
> $$
> 
> Thus the familiar formula from calculus still holds.



##### solution

For the principal branch of the logarithm, we write

$$
z=x+iy=re^{i\theta},
$$

where

$$
r=|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}
$$

and the principal argument satisfies

$$
-\pi<\theta<\pi.
$$

Thus the principal branch is

$$
\log z=\ln r+i\theta.
$$

This function is defined only when

$$
r>0
$$

and when the argument can be chosen continuously with

$$
-\pi<\theta<\pi.
$$

That excludes the origin and the negative real axis. Therefore the principal branch is defined on

$$
\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0].
$$

To show that it is analytic there, write

$$
\log z=u(x,y)+iv(x,y),
$$

where

$$
u(x,y)=\ln r=\frac12\ln(x^2+y^2)
$$

and

$$
v(x,y)=\operatorname{Arg} z.
$$

On the slit plane, the principal argument can be written piecewise as

$$
v(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\arctan(y/x), & x>0,\\[4pt]
\pi+\arctan(y/x), & x<0,\ y>0,\\[4pt]
-\pi+\arctan(y/x), & x<0,\ y<0.
\end{cases}
$$

The added constants $\pm\pi$ do not affect the partial derivatives, so throughout the slit plane we may compute the derivatives of $v$ from the $\arctan(y/x)$ part.

First compute the partial derivatives of $u$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac12\ln(x^2+y^2)\right)\\
&=\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{x^2+y^2}\cdot 2x\\
&=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2},
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_y
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac12\ln(x^2+y^2)\right)\\
&=\frac12\cdot\frac{1}{x^2+y^2}\cdot 2y\\
&=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now compute the partial derivatives of $v$. Since

$$
v(x,y)=\arctan(y/x)
$$

up to an additive constant on each piece of the domain, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_x
&=\frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{1+y^2/x^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right)\\
&=\frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right)\\
&=-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_y
&=\frac{1}{1+(y/x)^2}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{1+y^2/x^2}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\\
&=\frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\\
&=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
u_x=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}=v_y
$$

and

$$
u_y=\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}=-\left(-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\right)=-v_x.
$$

So the Cauchy-Riemann equations hold on

$$
\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0].
$$

The partial derivatives are continuous there, because their denominators are

$$
x^2+y^2,
$$

which never vanish on the slit plane. Hence, by the Cauchy-Riemann theorem, the principal branch of $\log z$ is analytic on

$$
\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0].
$$

To compute its derivative, use the formula

$$
f'(z)=u_x+iv_x.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dz}\log z
&=u_x+iv_x\\
&=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}+i\left(-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2}\right)\\
&=\frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now simplify:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2}
&=\frac{x-iy}{(x+iy)(x-iy)}\\
&=\frac{1}{x+iy}\\
&=\frac{1}{z}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{d}{dz}\log z=\frac{1}{z}.
$$

Thus the familiar calculus formula remains valid on the domain of analyticity of the principal branch.

$$

This has exactly the same local form as the principal branch of the logarithm; only the allowed interval for $\theta$ changes. Since the added branch constant changes the argument by integer multiples of $2\pi$, it does not change the partial derivatives. Therefore the same Cauchy-Riemann computation used for the principal branch applies on the slit plane for $\log_\alpha z$ as well, and shows that the function is analytic there.

The derivative is then computed in the same way:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dz}\log_\alpha z
&=u_x+iv_x\\
&=\frac{x-iy}{x^2+y^2}\\
&=\frac{1}{x+iy}\\
&=\frac{1}{z}.
\end{aligned}
$$

So every branch of the logarithm is analytic away from its branch cut, with derivative $1/z$.

##### problem 2

Let

Since the principal branch of $\log z$ is analytic on

\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0],

and the exponential function is entire, the composition

z^a=e^{a\log z}

If you write $\bar{z}=x-i y$, then $u_{x}=1, v_{y}=-1$, showing that the Cauchy-Riemann equations do not hold at any point. > [!exercise] Exercise 4: Failure of analyticity
> Use the Cauchy-Riemann equations to show that $f(z)=x^{2}+i(2 y+x)$ fails to be analytic at all points.



##### solution

Write

$$
f(z)=x^2+i(2y+x)
$$

in the form

$$
f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y),
$$

where

$$
u(x,y)=x^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v(x,y)=2y+x.
$$

Now compute the partial derivatives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_x&=2x,\\
u_y&=0,\\
v_x&=1,\\
v_y&=2.
\end{aligned}
$$

For analyticity, the Cauchy-Riemann equations would require

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

That is, we would need

$$
2x=2
$$

and

$$
0=-1.
$$

The second equation is impossible at every point. Therefore the Cauchy-Riemann equations fail everywhere.

Hence

$$
f(z)=x^2+i(2y+x)
$$

is not analytic at any point of $\mathbb C$.

> [!review]
> 1. If the Cauchy-Riemann equations hold at a single point of a function's domain, is the function necessarily analytic at that point?
> 2. If an analytic function has zero derivative throughout a region $\Omega$, what can be concluded about the function on $\Omega$ ? Prove your answer.
> 3. Is it possible for a non-constant analytic function to have zero derivative throughout its domain?
> 4. If two analytic functions on a region have the same real part, or the same imaginary part, what can be concluded about their relationship on the region? Prove your answer.



##### problem 1

No. If the Cauchy-Riemann equations hold at only a single point, that does not imply that the function is analytic at that point.

Analyticity is a local property on an open neighborhood. The Cauchy-Riemann equations must hold in a neighborhood of the point, together with the needed regularity assumptions, in order to conclude analyticity. So agreement at one isolated point is not enough.

##### problem 2

If an analytic function has zero derivative throughout a region

$$
\Omega,
$$

then the function is constant on $\Omega$.

To prove this, write

$$
f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y).
$$

Since

$$
f'(z)=0
$$

for every $z\in\Omega$, and since for an analytic function

$$
f'(z)=u_x+iv_x,
$$

we get

$$
u_x=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v_x=0
$$

throughout $\Omega$.

Because $f$ is analytic, the Cauchy-Riemann equations also hold:

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

Using

$$
u_x=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v_x=0,
$$

we obtain

$$
v_y=u_x=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
u_x=u_y=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v_x=v_y=0
$$

everywhere on $\Omega$.

So both the real part $u$ and the imaginary part $v$ have zero partial derivatives throughout the region. Hence neither $u$ nor $v$ changes as one moves through the connected set $\Omega$. Therefore both are constant on $\Omega$, and so

$$
f=u+iv
$$

is constant on $\Omega$.

##### problem 3

No. A non-constant analytic function cannot have zero derivative throughout its domain, provided the domain is a region.

Indeed, problem 2 shows that if an analytic function satisfies

$$
f'(z)=0
$$

everywhere on a region, then the function must be constant there. So zero derivative everywhere forces constancy.

The only way to have derivative zero everywhere and still not be constant is for the domain to fail to be connected. That is why connectedness is built into the definition of region and is essential in Theorem 2.

##### problem 4

If two analytic functions on a region have the same real part, or the same imaginary part, then they differ by a constant:

$$
f(z)=g(z)+c
$$

for some constant $c$.

To prove this, first suppose

$$
\operatorname{Re} f=\operatorname{Re} g
$$

on the region $\Omega$. Consider the difference

$$
h(z)=f(z)-g(z).
$$

Then $h$ is analytic on $\Omega$, and its real part is

$$
\operatorname{Re} h=\operatorname{Re} f-\operatorname{Re} g=0.
$$

So we may write

$$
h(z)=0+i\,v(x,y)=iv(x,y).
$$

Thus its real part is identically zero. For $h$, the Cauchy-Riemann equations become

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

But here

$$
u=0,
$$

so

$$
u_x=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=0.
$$

Hence

$$
v_y=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v_x=0.
$$

Therefore $v$ is constant on the region, so

$$
h(z)=ic
$$

for some real constant $c$. Equivalently,

$$
f(z)=g(z)+ic.
$$

This is of the form

$$
f(z)=g(z)+c
$$

with $c$ constant.

Now suppose instead that

$$
\operatorname{Im} f=\operatorname{Im} g.
$$

Then for the same difference

$$
h(z)=f(z)-g(z),
$$

we have

$$
\operatorname{Im} h=0.
$$

So write

$$
h(z)=u(x,y)+i0=u(x,y).
$$

Applying the Cauchy-Riemann equations to $h$ gives

$$
u_x=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=0,
$$

so $u$ is constant on the region. Therefore

$$
h(z)=c
$$

for some real constant $c$, and hence again

$$
f(z)=g(z)+c.
$$

So in either case, equality of real parts or equality of imaginary parts forces the two analytic functions to differ by a constant on the region.

The Cauchy-Riemann equations may hold only at one point (see Exercise 13 for an illustration). This does not imply that the function is analytic at that point, since analyticity requires that these equations be satisfied in a neighborhood.

The final applications show how the Cauchy-Riemann equations can be used to derive important results of theoretical nature.


> [!theorem] Theorem 2
> Suppose that $f(z)=u(x, y)+i v(x, y)$ is analytic in a region (open and connected) $\Omega$ and $f^{\prime}(z)=0$ for all $z$ in $\Omega$. Then $f$ is constant in $\Omega$.





The connectedness of $\Omega$ in the theorem is essential. For example, the function $f$, defined on the set $S$ in _Figure 3_, by

$$
f(z)= \begin{cases}1 & \text { if }|z|<2 \\ 0 & \text { if }|z|>3\end{cases}
$$

has zero derivative but is not constant.

\begin{aligned}

##### solution

##### problem 39(a)

We are given

$$
x=r\cos\theta
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
y=r\sin\theta.
$$

So the needed partial derivatives of $x$ and $y$ are

$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}=\cos\theta,
\qquad
\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}=\sin\theta,
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}=-r\sin\theta,
\qquad
\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta}=r\cos\theta.
$$

Now substitute these into the multivariable chain rule.

First,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}
&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}
 +\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}\\
&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\cos\theta
 +\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\sin\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
u_r=\cos\theta\,u_x+\sin\theta\,u_y.
$$

Next,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial \theta}
&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}
 +\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta}\\
&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(-r\sin\theta)
 +\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}(r\cos\theta).
\end{aligned}
$$

So

$$
u_\theta=-r\sin\theta\,u_x+r\cos\theta\,u_y.
$$

Similarly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial r}
&=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}
 +\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}\\
&=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\cos\theta
 +\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\sin\theta,
\end{aligned}
$$

so

$$
v_r=\cos\theta\,v_x+\sin\theta\,v_y.
$$

And

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial v}{\partial \theta}
&=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta}
 +\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta}\\
&=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}(-r\sin\theta)
 +\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}(r\cos\theta),
\end{aligned}
$$

so

$$
v_\theta=-r\sin\theta\,v_x+r\cos\theta\,v_y.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
u_r&=\cos\theta\,u_x+\sin\theta\,u_y,\\
u_\theta&=-r\sin\theta\,u_x+r\cos\theta\,u_y,\\
v_r&=\cos\theta\,v_x+\sin\theta\,v_y,\\
v_\theta&=-r\sin\theta\,v_x+r\cos\theta\,v_y.
\end{aligned}}
$$

##### problem 39(b)

Assume that $f=u+iv$ is analytic. Then the Cartesian Cauchy-Riemann equations hold:

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

We now derive the polar form.

Start with the formula for $u_r$ from part (a):

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_r
&=\cos\theta\,u_x+\sin\theta\,u_y\\
&=\cos\theta\,v_y+\sin\theta(-v_x)\\
&=\cos\theta\,v_y-\sin\theta\,v_x.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now compute $\dfrac{1}{r}v_\theta$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1}{r}v_\theta
&=\frac{1}{r}\left(-r\sin\theta\,v_x+r\cos\theta\,v_y\right)\\
&=-\sin\theta\,v_x+\cos\theta\,v_y\\
&=\cos\theta\,v_y-\sin\theta\,v_x.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
u_r=\frac{1}{r}v_\theta.
$$

Now start with the formula for $v_r$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_r
&=\cos\theta\,v_x+\sin\theta\,v_y\\
&=\cos\theta(-u_y)+\sin\theta\,u_x\\
&=-\cos\theta\,u_y+\sin\theta\,u_x.
\end{aligned}
$$

Next compute $-\dfrac{1}{r}u_\theta$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-\frac{1}{r}u_\theta
&=-\frac{1}{r}\left(-r\sin\theta\,u_x+r\cos\theta\,u_y\right)\\
&=-\left(-\sin\theta\,u_x+\cos\theta\,u_y\right)\\
&=\sin\theta\,u_x-\cos\theta\,u_y.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
v_r=-\frac{1}{r}u_\theta.
$$

So the polar form of the Cauchy-Riemann equations is

$$
\boxed{
u_r=\frac{1}{r}v_\theta
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
v_r=-\frac{1}{r}u_\theta.}
$$

##### problem 39(c)

From Theorem 1 we know that

$$
f'(z)=u_x+iv_x.
$$

We want to rewrite this in polar form.

Start from the formula for $u_r+iv_r$ from part (a):

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_r+iv_r
&=\left(\cos\theta\,u_x+\sin\theta\,u_y\right)
 +i\left(\cos\theta\,v_x+\sin\theta\,v_y\right)\\
&=\cos\theta\,(u_x+iv_x)+\sin\theta\,(u_y+iv_y).
\end{aligned}
$$

Now use the Cauchy-Riemann equations to simplify $u_y+iv_y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_y+iv_y
&=-v_x+i u_x\\
&=i(u_x+iv_x).
\end{aligned}
$$

Substitute this into the previous expression:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_r+iv_r
&=\cos\theta\,(u_x+iv_x)+\sin\theta\,i(u_x+iv_x)\\
&=(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)(u_x+iv_x)\\
&=e^{i\theta}(u_x+iv_x)\\
&=e^{i\theta}f'(z).
\end{aligned}
$$

Now divide both sides by $e^{i\theta}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(z)
&=e^{-i\theta}(u_r+iv_r).
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{f'(z)=e^{-i\theta}\left(u_r+iv_r\right).}
$$

\end{aligned}

v(r,\theta)=\theta.

u_r

##### problem 42

Use the principal branch of $\log z$, so

\qquad r>0,\quad -\pi<\theta<\pi.

\sin(x+i y)=\sin x\,\cosh y+i\cos x\,\sinh y.

With $x=\ln r$ and $y=\theta$, this gives

\frac{1}{r}v_\theta

> [!exercise] Exercise 17
> 43. Jacobian of a transformation. The Jacobian of the mapping $(x, y) \mapsto (u(x, y), v(x, y))$ is the following determinant:
> 
> $$
> J=\operatorname{det}\left|\begin{array}{ll}
> \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} \\
> \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}
> \end{array}\right| .
> $$
> 
> Suppose that $f=u+i v$ is analytic. Show that the Jacobian equals $\left|f^{\prime}(z)\right|^{2}$. You may recall from calculus of several variables that a mapping is locally invertible at every point where the Jacobian is nonzero. This exercise suggests that a similar result holds for $f$. Indeed, there is an inverse function theorem that states that if $f^{\prime}\left(z_{0}\right) \neq 0$, then in some neighborhood of $z_{0}, f$ is one-to-one and has an inverse function that is itself analytic in a neighborhood of $f\left(z_{0}\right)$ (see Section 5.7).




##### solution

Let

$$
f(z)=u(x,y)+i v(x,y),
$$

and suppose that $f$ is analytic. Since $f$ is analytic, the Cauchy-Riemann equations hold:

$$
u_x=v_y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$

The Jacobian of the mapping $(x,y)\mapsto (u(x,y),v(x,y))$ is

$$
J=\det
\begin{pmatrix}
u_x & u_y\\
v_x & v_y
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
J
&=u_x v_y-u_y v_x.
\end{aligned}
$$

Now use the Cauchy-Riemann equations. Since $v_y=u_x$ and $u_y=-v_x$, we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
J
&=u_x v_y-u_y v_x\\
&=u_x(u_x)-(-v_x)v_x\\
&=u_x^2+v_x^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

But for an analytic function we also know that

$$
f'(z)=u_x+i v_x.
$$

Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|f'(z)\right|^2
&=\left|u_x+i v_x\right|^2\\
&=u_x^2+v_x^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Combining the two computations gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
J
&=u_x^2+v_x^2\\
&=\left|f'(z)\right|^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{J=\left|f'(z)\right|^2.}
$$

If we instead use the equivalent formula $f'(z)=v_y-i u_y$, then we obtain the same result in the form

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|f'(z)\right|^2
&=\left|v_y-i u_y\right|^2\\
&=v_y^2+u_y^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

and the Cauchy-Riemann equations give

$$
\begin{aligned}
J
&=u_x v_y-u_y v_x\\
&=v_y^2+u_y^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

So the identity is consistent from either point of view: the Jacobian of an analytic mapping is the square of the modulus of its derivative.
